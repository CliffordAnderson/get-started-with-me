Get started with...

Short lessons in artificial intelligence, each built around one idea small enough to hold in your mind and experiment with.

Each 10–15 minutes to read.

1
perceptrons

What a single artificial neuron computes, why its answer is always a straight line drawn through the space of its inputs, and how it finds that line from labelled examples by correcting its own mistakes.

Roughly ten minutes. You place the examples and run the learning rule on them.
2
cats and dogs

The same unit as the first lesson, handed 144 inputs and a pile of small pictures. What its weights look like when you fold them back into an image, why that image is the whole of what it knows, and the two-pixel nudge that takes it apart.

Roughly twelve minutes. You train it, read its template, then break it.
3
exclusive or

The smallest function a single perceptron cannot compute, the argument that proves it, and what happens to the space when you put a second layer in the way.

Roughly 15 minutes. You try to draw the line yourself before finding out why it is impossible.
4
backpropagation

Where the weights in the last lesson came from. A smooth unit has a slope you can walk down, and the chain rule shares the blame backwards through a hidden layer nobody ever told what to say — along with the guarantee that is lost on the way.

Roughly 15 minutes. You walk the error surface by hand, then let two hundred restarts settle how often it works.
5
expert systems

A machine whose knowledge is a list of sentences you can read, disagree with and correct — the road the field took while the perceptron was out of favour. It works backwards from the question, attaches a certainty to its answer, shows its derivation on request, and falls off a cliff at the edge of what it was told.

Roughly fifteen minutes. You run a consultation, read the derivation, then take it outside its rules.
6
reinforcement learning

A machine given no labelled examples and no rules — only a number that arrives at the end of a journey, saying nothing about which of the moves before it earned the credit. It learns a route anyway, and then walks past something twice as good, one step away, for ever.

Roughly fifteen minutes. You walk the world yourself, watch the table fill in, then put a better prize where it will never look.
7
language models

A machine that learns from nothing but text — no labels, no rules, no reward — by counting which word follows which in one novel, Kafka's The Trial. What the counts can write, when writing becomes quoting, and what they can say about the one chapter they never read.

Roughly fifteen minutes. You play the guessing game, read the table, then hand it a chapter it has never seen.
