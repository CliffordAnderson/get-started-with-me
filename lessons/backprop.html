A slope to follow

The last lesson built a network that computes XOR and then handed you its weights, already set. This one throws them away. Every number here starts as a small random one, and the network finds the fold on its own — by measuring how wrong it is and walking downhill.

1

A dial instead of a switch

To walk downhill you need a hill. The threshold unit of the first three lessons does not give you one: its answer is 0 or 1 and nothing in between, so the error it makes sits on a flat step and stays there however you nudge the weights, right up to the moment it jumps. Replace the switch with a dial — a sigmoid, which slides smoothly from 0 to 1 — and the same error becomes a surface with a slope you can read off.

The unit below has two inputs and is being asked for AND. Its second weight and its bias are held still; you may move the first. Watch the error as you do.

The staircase is not a poor slope; it is no slope at all. On every tread the derivative is exactly zero, so a rule that asks which way is down is told nothing, and at the two risers it is undefined. The perceptron rule sidesteps this by not asking — it moves the weights by a fixed recipe whenever the answer comes out wrong — but that recipe needs to know which unit was wrong, and in a network with a hidden layer nobody tells the hidden units what they should have said.

The sigmoid's valley has its lowest point at w₁ = 1.00, which is the middle of the flat tread where the step unit gets all four cases right. The smooth version is not solving a different problem; it is solving the same one in a way that leaves a trail to follow. Note also that its error there is 0.230 rather than zero — a sigmoid never quite reaches 0 or 1, so a correct answer is always a confident approximation and never an exact one.

2

The error surface

Free the second weight as well and the curve becomes a landscape: one height, the error, over every pair of weights the unit could hold. Learning is now a matter of standing somewhere on it and repeatedly stepping in the direction that goes down fastest. That direction is the gradient, and for a single smooth unit it costs almost nothing to work out.

Bernard Widrow and Marcian Hoff, “Adaptive Switching Circuits,” IRE WESCON Convention Record (1960), introduced this least-mean-squares rule for a single adaptive element they built in hardware and called ADALINE. It is the perceptron's exact contemporary, and it descends an error surface rather than counting mistakes — but for one unit only. Reaching through a hidden layer took another twenty-six years to become common knowledge.

The step size matters more than it looks. Small and the descent is faithful but slow; too large and each step overshoots the valley floor and lands part of the way up the far side, so the error rises on the very move that was meant to lower it. Anything up to about 8 on this surface arrives exactly at the bottom; from 10 upward the marker never settles at all, crossing and re-crossing the valley for as long as you let it run. It does not fly off to infinity — the sigmoid flattens out at the edges and holds it in — it simply circles, and nothing in the rule notices or corrects for that.

This surface is smooth and has a single lowest point, so any rate small enough to be stable arrives at the same place. That property belongs to the single unit, not to the method. It is the first thing lost when a hidden layer is added, and the last panel is about what is lost with it.

3

Sharing out the blame

Now the network from the last lesson: two inputs, two hidden units, one output, every activation a sigmoid. The output unit's error is plain enough — it answered y and should have answered t. The hidden units are the difficulty. No one ever said what they should have answered, so their share of the mistake has to be worked out from the output's, passed back along the very weights that carried their answers forward.

That is the whole of the idea. A hidden unit that fed a large weight into a badly wrong output is largely to blame; one whose weight was near zero is not, whatever it did. Choose a case and read the two passes.

The second line is the one that does the work, and it is only the chain rule written out. The hidden unit's blame is the output's blame, multiplied by the weight along which its answer travelled, multiplied by how much its own output would have moved had its input moved — which is h(1 − h), and which is nearly zero when the unit is already saturated near 0 or 1. A confident hidden unit is a deaf one. That factor is where the step function's flatness has gone: it has not been abolished, only softened into something that is merely small rather than exactly zero.

Nothing above is specific to two layers. The same two lines applied again reach a third layer, and again a fourth, each one multiplying in one more weight and one more slope. That is the whole method, and also its long-running weakness: a product of many small factors is a very small number, and a hidden unit twenty layers back can be told almost nothing about what it did wrong.

4

Finding the fold

Put the two passes in a loop over all four cases and let it run. The last lesson set the hidden weights by hand so that both units counted how many inputs were on, one firing at a count of one, the other only at two — which folded the awkward pair onto a single point. Nobody is going to tell this network about that. Watch where it puts the four cases instead.

Distance in hidden space between the images of (0,1) and (1,0): measured once training has moved.

Watch the trace rather than the epoch counter. A run usually sheds a little error early on, then hangs for a long stretch, moving so slowly that it looks finished, before dropping steeply near the end. It is tempting to say that the flat stretch is the two hidden units sitting identical until the fold appears, but that is not what the weights show — they have already drifted well apart by then, and the stall is something less tidy. Nothing schedules the shape, and no part of the rule knows about it. It falls out of the surface the weights are walking on.

Train it a few times over and the solutions it finds will not all be the same. Sometimes it recovers the neat arrangement from the last lesson, with the two awkward cases landing on the same point; often it finds some other corner of hidden space where a line happens to work, with the four cases spread out and no tidy interpretation available. Both compute XOR. Only one of them was the answer anybody would have written down, and the method has no preference between them.

5

What descent does not promise

Rosenblatt's rule came with a theorem: if a separating line exists, the perceptron will find it, in a finite number of corrections, from any starting weights. Gradient descent through a hidden layer has no such guarantee and cannot have one. The surface is no longer a single valley, and a method that only ever moves downhill stops wherever the ground is level — whether or not that place is any good.

The failures nearly all look alike, which is what makes them interesting. The network does fold the space — but it folds the wrong pair together. Which pair varies from one stuck run to the next; that the two cases disagree about the answer almost never does. Once they sit on the same point in hidden space they are a single input as far as the output unit is concerned, and no weights on it can pull them apart again. So it answers close to 0.50 to both, which is half a mistake each and the best available without unfolding first. Unfolding means going uphill, and the method will not go uphill.

The second button is a different failure and a shorter one. Start every weight at zero and the two hidden units are identical; being identical, they receive identical blame; receiving identical blame, they take identical steps and stay identical for ever. The gradient is not wrong, and the network never moves. Random initialisation is not a convenience here, it is what breaks the symmetry.

None of this stopped the method from working. It stops it from being a theorem, which is a different complaint and the one Minsky and Papert actually made. What was found in the 1980s was not a proof that layered learning converges — there is none — but that in practice, on problems people cared about, it converged often enough to be worth the trouble, and that the trouble scaled.

David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams, “Learning Representations by Back-Propagating Errors,” Nature 323 (1986), is the paper that made the method common property, and it is candid about the local minima. The rule itself had been arrived at before, by Seppo Linnainmaa (1970) as a general method for the accumulated rounding error of a composite function, and by Paul Werbos (1974) for exactly this purpose in a doctoral thesis that went largely unread.
