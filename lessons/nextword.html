lesson 7 · language models

The next word

Every machine so far was given something along with its examples: a label on each one, a rule from an expert, a reward at the end of a journey. This one is given a book — Kafka's The Trial, which travels with this page — and nothing else. No part of the book is marked right or wrong; it is simply what was written, one word after another. The machine's whole task is to answer one question: given the words so far, which word comes next? A machine that answers it is a language model, and this lesson builds the plainest one there is, out of counting.

words written 0

Each word is drawn from a count of what followed the two words before it, somewhere in the novel.

A short guide to the numbers

Everything on this page reduces to counts: how many times one thing followed another in a fixed text. A proportion is one count divided by another, and every percentage here is such a proportion, measured, not assumed.

Drawing in proportion to the counts means choosing at random, but with each option's chance set by its count. If one word followed a context 74 times and another 60, the first is drawn with chance 74 out of 134. Repeated draws are what generate the text on this page.

Before you try: How often do you expect to name the exact next word of a novel?

1

Guess the next word

Cover the next word of a book and try to name it. You will be right more often than seems reasonable, because ordinary prose is redundant: grammar rules out most words, the subject rules out most of the rest. The machine playing against you does none of that reasoning. It counts.

Type the word you expect, then reveal. Spelling counts; capitals and punctuation do not.

The passage is cut from the first nine chapters of the novel. When you reveal, the counts guess too: the word that most often follows the two words before the gap, in this book. That is the entire method, and it is the same method every later section uses.

Keep one fact in view while you compare scores: the counts have read this exact book, this exact sentence included. What that advantage is worth — and what is left of it on a page the counts have never met — is measured in the last section.

Claude Shannon, “Prediction and Entropy of Printed English,” Bell System Technical Journal 30 (1951), had readers guess a text letter by letter and used their scores to estimate how predictable English is — about one bit of genuine surprise per letter, by his figures, with wide error bounds. This panel is the word-level version of his experiment.

Takeaway: Ordinary text is redundant enough that the next word can often be predicted, by a reader or by a count.

2

A model made of counting

Here is the whole method. Read the novel once and, for every context — the last word, or the last two, or three — write down what came next. Chapters one to nine hold 81,571 words; the one-word contexts fill a table of 8,670 rows, the two-word contexts one of 44,143. To predict, look the context up and take the biggest count. To write, draw from the counts at random, in proportion. There is nothing else.

 

One, two or three words. Anything from the novel has a row; anything else does not.

P(word | context) = count(context, word) / count(context)

“the” occurs 4,341 times, followed most often by “lawyer”, “door” and “court”. A context and its follower together are called an n-gram, and a model that predicts from their counts is an n-gram model. The bookkeeping is deliberately crude: a word here is anything between two spaces, so “court” and “court,” are two different words. Nothing in this lesson needs anything finer.

Try contexts of your own. Two words already have opinions: “he was” is followed by “not” more than by anything else, and everything “said the” leads to is a person. The table knows no grammar and no meaning. It has met every one of these words before, in order, and that is all it has.

A. A. Markov, in a paper read to the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences in 1913, counted pairs of letters through twenty thousand letters of Pushkin's Eugene Onegin — vowel or consonant — to show that dependent events could still be treated statistically. Counting what follows what in a fixed text begins there.

Takeaway: The model is a table of counts; everything it knows about language is which words followed which, and how often.

3

It learns spelling first

Words are not special. Run the same table over single characters — chapters one to nine use 66 distinct ones, counting the space, the capitals and the punctuation — and let the context be the last few characters instead of the last few words. Then write, one character at a time, and watch what each added character of context buys.

3

 

At zero context every character is drawn by its overall frequency, and the output is not even words. One character of context teaches it which letters follow which; by three or four it spells, mostly; around five the words are nearly all real ones; at seven it holds short phrases together and the register is unmistakably this novel's. Nothing taught it spelling. Spelling is a regularity of the text, and counting inherits every regularity it is deep enough to reach.

Watch the readout under the figure as much as the text. The table has 66 rows at one character of context, 5,893 at three, and 149,419 at seven. The quality is being bought with rows, and the next section is about where that price ends up.

Claude Shannon, “A Mathematical Theory of Communication,” Bell System Technical Journal 27 (1948), constructs exactly this series of letter-level approximations to English, and measures how much each added letter of context reduces the uncertainty of the next. The paper founded information theory; the approximations occupy two of its pages.

Takeaway: Longer context makes counted text more like its source; even spelling is only a statistical regularity of the text.

Before you try: If a four-word context occurred only once in the novel, what must follow it?

4

When counting becomes quoting

Longer context makes better text, so why not take more of it? Because the table only has rows for contexts that actually occur, and the longer a context is, the fewer times it occurs. Of the two-word contexts, 81 per cent were only ever followed by one thing. At three words it is 94 per cent; at four, 98.5. A model whose every draw is forced is not writing. It is reciting.

  • rows in the table—
  • contexts with one continuation—
  • six-word windows found in the novel—

At one word of context the output is fresh nonsense: real words, no thread, and almost no six-word run of it anywhere in Kafka. At two, the text begins to hold together, and about a fifth of its six-word windows turn out to be verbatim quotation. At three, roughly five windows in six are; at four the model is reading the novel back to you with occasional wrong turnings.

This is the table's dilemma, and it is worth stating exactly. The settings that produce English are the settings that produce quotation, because the only way the table can continue a long context is the way the book continued it. A larger corpus pushes the trade further out — with a million books, four-word contexts stop being unique — but it does not change its shape. However much text is counted, coherence keeps asking for longer context, and longer contexts keep running out of examples.

Writes 2,000 words at the selected context length and checks every six-word window of them against the novel.

Measure each context length and the curve says what the shaded samples already suggested. The rise is steep because uniqueness arrives quickly: most three-word phrases of an ordinary novel occur in it exactly once. The counts do not gradually become derivative. They cross from babble to quotation in the space of two added words, and no setting between the two writes new, sensible prose.

Nothing here is specific to Kafka. Any single book gives the same curve, shifted a little by its length and its habits of repetition. What the table lacks is not more Kafka; it is any way of continuing a context it has not seen — which is the subject of the last section.

Takeaway: As context grows, most contexts become unique, so faithful continuation turns into quotation.

Before you try: What can the table say about a chapter whose word pairs it never counted?

5

The chapter it never read

Every measurement so far was made on the chapters the model counted. The novel has a tenth chapter — a short one, in which K.'s trial ends — and not one count from it entered any table on this page. It is a held-out set, and it asks the only question that separates learning from memorising: does the table know anything beyond the pages it read?

Walks all 81,571 words of chapters one to nine and all 2,087 of chapter ten, asking at each position whether the context has a row and whether the biggest count names the actual next word.

On its own chapters the three-word table looks close to perfect: it names the exact next word about nine times in ten. That number does not survive the page turn. On chapter ten it finds its context in the table about once in seven tries, and names the word right about once in fifty. The two-word table generalises a little better and still collapses; even single words fail, because 172 of chapter ten's 2,087 words occur nowhere in the first nine chapters.

Nine in ten was never a measure of what the table knows about prose. It was a measure of how much of the training text a three-word context pins down — the same fact section 4 measured as quotation, seen from the other side. The difference between the two columns is the difference between memorising and generalising, and it is why every honest score in these lessons has come from held-out data.

It will look up your last three words, then two, then one, and continue from the first row it finds.

Hand it your own words and watch what failure looks like. When your last words occur in the novel, it continues fluently. When they do not, it drops context until something matches, trading away what you said for the ability to say anything at all. And when your final word is one the novel never uses, it produces nothing — not a poor guess, nothing. A table has no notion of a near miss. This is the cliff at the edge of the rules from lesson 5, reached from the other side: those rules were written and ended where the writing stopped, these counts were read and end where the reading stopped, and the brittleness at the edge is the same.

The fix is the one that closed the last lesson. The table would have to become a function that guesses what follows a context it has never seen — one that has learned, from the contexts it did see, that “lawyer” and “painter” turn up in the same places, so that counting one teaches it a little about the other. The next lesson takes the first step: it counts, from these same nine chapters, which words keep the same company, and measures what that similarity buys back on chapter ten. A learned function trained on the next-word question, at a scale that deserves a lesson of its own, is what today's language models are.

Takeaway: A table predicts only what it has counted; text it has never seen needs generalisation, which counting does not provide.
