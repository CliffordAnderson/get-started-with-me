Exclusive or

Exclusive or is the or of ordinary speech. Tea or coffee offers you one of them, not both. The plain OR of logic is inclusive and accepts both; the exclusive version rules that case out. XOR answers 1 when exactly one of its two inputs is on, and 0 when the inputs agree — whether they agree on 1 or on 0.

Flip the inputs. OR and XOR answer alike in three of the four cases and part company on the last, where both inputs are on.

Two other readings of the same function are worth carrying forward. XOR reports whether its inputs differ, which makes it a comparator. And it reports their parity — whether an odd number of them are on — which is the reading that survives past two inputs.

None of this is exotic. XOR is the sum bit of binary addition, with AND supplying the carry; it is the parity bit that catches a flipped bit in transmission; it is what a one-time pad applies to a message. What makes it awkward for a perceptron is narrow and specific. Of the sixteen functions of two binary inputs, fourteen can be settled by a single weighted threshold. XOR and its negation are the two that cannot.

2

The problem, as Minsky and Papert posed it

A perceptron in their sense is not a single unit but a small machine: an array of input points, a layer of predicates that each see only some limited part of it, and one threshold unit that takes a weighted vote of what those predicates report. Their question was how much of the input a single predicate must see before that vote can come out right. They called that number the order of the function.

AND, OR and majority have order 1 — one point apiece is enough. XOR does not, and it is the light case of a heavy result: parity, whether an odd number of inputs are on, has an order equal to the entire input. No quantity of local evidence, however it is weighted, settles it. XOR is the version you can draw.

Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert, Perceptrons: An Introduction to Computational Geometry (MIT Press, 1969; expanded edition 1988). The order results run through Part I; parity and connectedness, not XOR, are the book's real subjects.

What the unit actually does

A perceptron multiplies each input by a weight, adds a bias, and fires when the total clears zero. The weights tilt the boundary, the bias slides it — but the boundary is always a straight line, and that is the whole of what the unit can represent. Choose a target and try to place it.

Let it train itself

The perceptron learning rule repairs mistakes one case at a time: when the unit answers wrongly, push the weights a step toward the right answer. Rosenblatt proved this always terminates — as long as some separating line exists. Run it on a function that has one, then on the one that doesn't.

The reason, in four lines

The failure isn't a matter of searching harder. Read each row of the XOR truth table as a demand on the weights and the four demands turn out to contradict each other.

  1. b < 0from x = (0,0), which must output 0
  2. w₁ + b ≥ 0from x = (1,0), which must output 1
  3. w₂ + b ≥ 0from x = (0,1), which must output 1
  4. w₁ + w₂ + b < 0from x = (1,1), which must output 0
  5. w₁ + w₂ + 2b ≥ 0add the second and third lines
  6. w₁ + w₂ + b ≥ −b > 0move one b across; the first line says −b is positive
  7. w₁ + w₂ + b is both positive and negativewhich no three numbers can arrange

The same argument works for any threshold, any sign convention, any scaling of the weights. Nothing about the unit is under-powered; the demands are simply inconsistent.

Give it a place to bend

Put two units between the inputs and the output. Each one is still just a straight cut — nothing has become more expressive on its own. What changes is that their two outputs become the coordinates the last unit reads. Slide the control and watch where the four cases end up.

h₁ = step(x₁ + x₂ − 0.5) h₂ = step(x₁ + x₂ − 1.5)

Both hidden units read the same quantity: how many inputs are on. One of them fires at a count of one or more, the other only at two. Between them they turn a count into a two-bit code — 00, 10, 11 — and in that code the two awkward cases, (0,1) and (1,0), land in exactly the same place. Once they are the same point, no line has to separate them from each other.

Set the activation to none and the fold disappears. Two linear maps compose into one linear map, so the network collapses back to a single cut and the ceiling returns to three. The layer is not what bought the extra power. The bend inside it did.

7

What the second layer changed

A single-layer perceptron can ask only one question: which side of a line is this point on? Whatever cannot be split that way is outside what the architecture expresses, and no amount of training reaches it. The limit belongs to the model, not to the optimizer.

A hidden layer with a nonlinearity between the layers lets the network re-plot its inputs before the final cut. Each hidden unit still contributes a straight boundary, but the region they carve out jointly can be a corner, a stripe, or an island. Enough hidden units and you can approximate any reasonable decision region to whatever tolerance you like.

The book is more careful than its reputation. Minsky and Papert observed in passing that a universal computer could be assembled out of linear threshold units, so the power of layered machines was never the question. What they doubted, in a short section near the end, was that a learning theory as clean as the perceptron rule would extend to them — our intuitive judgment that the extension is sterile, a judgment they invited their readers to refute.

The step function is part of the difficulty: its gradient is zero almost everywhere, so nothing tells a hidden unit which way to lean. Replacing it with a smooth function, as the sigmoid setting above does, restores that signal, and backpropagation (Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, 1986) uses it to assign credit through the hidden layer. The two-unit XOR network is the smallest case where that machinery earns its keep, and the next lesson runs it — the same two hidden units and the same fold, but with every weight found rather than given.
