lesson 5 · expert systems

A machine you can argue with

Everything the last four lessons built keeps its knowledge as numbers, and the numbers are unreadable. This one — an expert system, in the language of the 1970s — keeps its knowledge as sentences. You can read every one of them, disagree with any of them, and follow exactly how it reached its answer — and it still fails, for reasons that have nothing to do with a boundary in the wrong place.

15 rules

Running a consultation from the top.

1

Knowledge you can read

A rule is an IF / THEN sentence about the world, with a number on the end saying how strongly the one supports the other. Here is the entire rule base of the machine in this lesson — fifteen sentences, all of them in front of you. That is the whole of what it knows.

Compare it with the first lesson. That perceptron also knew exactly three numbers, and you could print them; but knowing that w₁ = 0.63 tells you nothing you can argue with. Here the knowledge is in a language you already speak. If rule 9 is wrong, you can say so, and say why, and change it — and nothing else in the machine needs retraining, because nothing in the machine was trained.

This is the trade the whole approach is built on. It was called knowledge engineering: sit with an expert, get the rules out of them, write the rules down. What the machine knows is what somebody told it — and the program that applies the rules, the inference engine, knows nothing about medicine whatever.

These fifteen rules are a teaching reconstruction in the shape of MYCIN's, not MYCIN's own. The real system had several hundred, written and revised over years with infectious disease specialists at Stanford. Nothing here is medical advice, and the therapies named are the ones a program of the middle 1970s would have reached for.

2

Working backwards

Nobody runs the rules forwards from everything known, because most of what could be concluded is beside the point. The machine starts from the question it was asked — what therapy? — and works back. To recommend a therapy it needs the organism; to get the organism it needs the kind of meningitis and the Gram stain; and when it runs out of rules for something, it asks you. That is backward chaining, and it is why a consultation feels like an interrogation.

That is the payoff of working backwards, and it is also the first thing that makes the machine feel intelligent when it is not: it asks a small number of pointed questions, in an order that looks like judgement, and the order is nothing but the shape of the tree above.

3

Why it said that

Ask the second lesson's classifier why it called a picture a dog and the only honest answer is a grid of 144 numbers. Ask this machine and it can hand you the derivation: this, because of that rule, whose premise held because of these two facts, one of which you told it. MYCIN could do this in 1975, and it mattered more than the diagnosis — the doctors would not use a program that could not show its work.

    Run a consultation in the previous panel to see its derivation.

    This is the property the approach was really selling, and the reason it is still argued about. The machine is not just accountable in principle — you cannot merely say that its behaviour is determined by its rules, the way a network's behaviour is determined by its weights. You can be handed the specific sentences responsible for this answer, on this case, and go and check them against a textbook.

    Edward Shortliffe, Randall Davis, Stanton Axline, Bruce Buchanan, C. Cordell Green and Stanley Cohen, “Computer-Based Consultations in Clinical Therapeutics: Explanation and Rule Acquisition Capabilities of the MYCIN System,” Computers and Biomedical Research 8 (1975), is where the explanation facility is set out. That it appears in the title, alongside rule acquisition and ahead of any claim about accuracy, is worth noticing.

    It is worth being exact about what the derivation does and does not settle. It tells you faithfully how this answer followed from these rules. It says nothing at all about whether the rules are right. A rule base can be perfectly transparent and perfectly wrong, and the tree will explain the wrong answer with the same clarity as the right one — a point the fifth panel makes on purpose.

    The contrast with a trained network is real, but it is narrower than it is usually made to sound. What you gain is not correctness. It is the ability to locate a disagreement in a particular sentence, which is what makes an argument possible at all.

    4

    Not quite probability

    Medicine is not made of certainties, so every rule carries a number: a certainty factor between −1 and +1, where +1 is settled truth, −1 settled falsity and 0 no evidence either way. Two rules that reach the same conclusion have their certainties combined. The arithmetic is not Bayes, and its authors knew it was not.

    +0.60
    +0.50

    …

    The rules are sensible enough. Two pieces of evidence pointing the same way reinforce each other with diminishing returns, so belief creeps towards 1 without arriving. Evidence pointing opposite ways partly cancels. A premise is only as strong as its weakest conjunct, so AND takes the minimum. And a rule whose premise is under 0.2 does not fire at all, which stops the machine from accumulating a strong conclusion out of a heap of faint suggestions.

    What the scheme buys is modularity, and that is the whole reason it exists. Each rule carries its own number, elicited from an expert on its own, and the numbers combine without anyone ever having to write down how all the evidence is jointly distributed. Asking a physician for a full joint distribution over dozens of findings was not a thing that could be done in 1975, or now.

    What it costs is that the numbers do not mean what they look like they mean. A certainty factor is a measure of how much the evidence shifts belief, not of how likely something is, and the combining rule is exact only when the pieces of evidence are conditionally independent given the hypothesis — which, for two findings on the same patient, they generally are not. Feed it correlated evidence and it will happily double-count.

    The model is set out in Edward Shortliffe and Bruce Buchanan, “A Model of Inexact Reasoning in Medicine,” Mathematical Biosciences 23 (1975). David Heckerman, “Probabilistic Interpretations for MYCIN's Certainty Factors” (1986), later worked out the conditions under which the scheme agrees with probability theory, and they are restrictive. The authors of MYCIN were explicit from the start that certainty factors were not probabilities.

    5

    The edge of the rule base

    The second lesson broke its classifier by moving two pixels. This machine breaks differently, and worse. Hand it a case its rules were never written for and it does not hesitate, hedge, or report that it is out of its depth. It answers, in the same tone, with the same derivation, at whatever certainty the arithmetic happens to produce.

    What it concluded

      This is brittleness, and it is the characteristic failure of the whole family. A trained network degrades: push it away from what it saw and its confidence usually sags along with its accuracy, which is at least a signal. A rule base has no gradient to sag along. Inside its coverage it is often excellent. One step outside, it is not a worse version of itself; it is a machine confidently applying rules about bacteria to a patient who has a fungus, because no sentence in it says that fungi exist.

      Nothing in the design can fix this from the inside. The machine cannot know what it has not been told, and it has no representation of the boundary of its own competence — there is no rule that says if none of the above, stop talking, and even if you wrote one, you would have to know in advance what the above failed to cover.

      The usual answer was to write more rules, which works, at a price that grows. Every new case at the margin is another interview, another sentence, another chance for the new sentence to interact with the six hundred already there in a way nobody predicted. The rule base is readable one rule at a time and quite unreadable all at once.

      That is the honest shape of the trade against a learned model, and it is not a rout in either direction. One of them is legible and cannot generalise. The other generalises and cannot be read. The forty years since have mostly been spent discovering that you would like both.

      6

      What it cost, and what it was worth

      The machine in this lesson took an afternoon to write because its fifteen rules are made up. MYCIN's were not. Every one of them came out of a conversation with somebody who knew something, and getting them out was the expensive part — expensive enough that the field gave it a name and treated it as the central obstacle.

      The knowledge acquisition bottleneck is the plain observation that experts cannot simply state what they know. They can decide cases fluently and explain any particular decision, and turn out, when pressed for the general rule, to produce something they immediately want to qualify. Extracting a few hundred usable rules from that is years of work with a person whose time is scarce, and it has to be redone as the field moves. MYCIN's knowledge of antimicrobials began going out of date the moment it was written.

      Set against that: it worked. In a blinded evaluation published in 1979, eight specialists in infectious disease judged therapy recommendations for ten meningitis cases, not knowing which came from the program and which from the nine human prescribers. MYCIN's recommendations were rated acceptable 65% of the time; the five faculty specialists scored between 42.5% and 62.5%.

      It was never used on a patient. Not because of the evaluation, but because a consultation meant sitting at a terminal wired to a Stanford mainframe and typing answers for half an hour, because nobody had solved who would be liable if it were wrong, and because the knowledge base would have needed a permanent staff to keep current. The obstacles were of their period, and the first of them dissolved entirely within twenty years.

      What the programme established was narrower and more durable than the AI-in-medicine headlines it produced: that a machine could reach expert-level conclusions in a real domain by manipulating symbols, and could show its working while it did — and that the cost of telling it everything it needed to know was the thing that would stop you.

      Edward Shortliffe, Computer-Based Medical Consultations: MYCIN (Elsevier, 1976), is the system itself, written as his Stanford doctoral thesis under Bruce Buchanan and Stanley Cohen. The evaluation is Victor Yu, Lawrence Fagan, Sharon Wraith et al., “Antimicrobial Selection by a Computer: A Blinded Evaluation by Infectious Diseases Experts,” JAMA 242 (1979) — ten cases, which is a small evaluation, and the authors present it as one. Rule counts differ by source and by year, since the base grew throughout the project; figures of roughly 450 and roughly 600 are both commonly given.
