lesson 9 · learned word vectors

A column for every word

The last lesson counted a profile for every word and found that nearness between profiles does work a table of counts cannot. Those profiles were counted once and then fixed. This lesson gets word vectors the other way: it hands the network from lesson 4 the next-word question from lesson 7, and lets backpropagation decide what the numbers should be. The first thing to settle is how a word gets into a machine that only multiplies numbers.

slot — of 600

Type any of the 600 commonest words in chapters one to nine of the novel.

A short guide to the numbers

A word arrives as 600 numbers: zero in every slot but its own, where it is one. Multiplying that vector by a table of weights adds up 600 rows with 599 of them multiplied by zero, so the answer is simply the one row belonging to that word. That row is the word's vector — sixteen numbers here — and it is a weight like any other weight in the network.

The network reads two words, adds up their rows through sixteen weights each into 32 sigmoid units, and turns those into a score for each of the 600 words it could say next. The scores become probabilities by the softmax: raise each to a power of e and divide by the total, so they are positive and add to one.

Training measures surprise: how many halvings of certainty the true next word cost, which is zero when the network was sure and large when it was blindsided. That number in bits is what the curve in section 3 plots. Six bits of surprise per word means the network was, on average, as unsure as someone choosing evenly among sixty-four words.

Before you try: The last lesson found that “lawyer” and “painter” keep similar company. Encoded this way, how similar should the two look?

1

How a word gets in

Every machine in this course multiplies numbers by weights and adds them up. A word is not a number, so before anything else something has to be agreed: which numbers stand for “lawyer”. The plainest answer available is also the one nearly every language model still starts from, and it has a consequence worth seeing before it is repaired.

  • slots the two share—
  • similarity of the two encodings—
  • distance between them—

Every pair of different words gives the same three numbers.

Give each word a slot. The vocabulary here is the 600 words used most often in chapters one to nine of Kafka's The Trial, the novel lessons 7 and 8 counted from, so a word is a list of 600 numbers: one in its own slot, zero everywhere else. Nothing has been decided about the word by doing this. The slot numbers come from an alphabetical or a frequency ordering, and neither carries meaning; only the position matters, and only because it differs from every other position.

That is the consequence. Two different words never fill the same slot, so the product of their two lists is always zero and the distance between them is always the square root of two. “lawyer” and “painter” are exactly as far apart as “lawyer” and “the”. The encoding asserts that all 600 words are mutually unrelated, in equal measure, and it asserts this before any text has been read. Lesson 8 described the count table's words as islands; this is that island written as a vector.

Two limits are being set here rather than discovered, and both come back in section 6. A vocabulary of 600 is a choice: the novel uses 5,082 distinct words, and every one of the other 4,482 has no slot at all, so it cannot be presented to this network in any form. And the vector says nothing, which means everything the network ends up knowing about a word has to be learned and stored somewhere else.

Takeaway: A one-hot encoding gives every word a slot and nothing else, so it makes all words equally unrelated before any text is read.

Before you try: If the input is 599 zeros and a single one, how much of the first weight matrix does any one word actually touch?

2

The first weight matrix is a table of vectors

The vector goes into a layer of weights, and that layer is where the lesson turns. Because the input is almost all zeros, the multiplication does something much simpler than it looks, and the weights on the other side of it stop being an implementation detail and become the object of interest.

  • rows in the table600
  • numbers in a row16
  • weights the selected word touches—

The sixteen numbers of the selected row are this word's vector.

Multiply a one-hot vector by a matrix and you are adding up 600 rows, 599 of which have been multiplied by zero. The answer is the remaining row, unchanged. So the matrix has one row per word, sixteen numbers wide, and passing a word through the first layer is a table lookup wearing the clothes of a multiplication. Nothing about it is a special mechanism; it is the ordinary layer of weights from lesson 1 with a very sparse input.

The consequence is the point of this lesson. Those sixteen numbers are weights, and weights are what gradient descent adjusts. Lesson 8's profiles were counted from the text and then left alone; these are parameters, and the same rule that moved the hidden weights in lesson 4 moves them. Whatever a word ends up meaning to this network is written in its row, and it is written there by training, not by counting.

Until section 3 has run, the picture above is of the small random numbers the network was initialised with, and no two of them mean anything; the line under it says which of the two states you are looking at. What section 3 does is run backpropagation against a question, and what section 4 does is come back and read this same table afterwards.

Takeaway: A one-hot input turns the first weight matrix into a lookup table with one row of learnable numbers per word.

Before you try: Nobody will tell the network that “said” and “asked” are alike. What could make it put similar numbers in their two rows anyway?

3

Filling the table in

The table has to be filled by training, and training needs a question. Lesson 7 has one: given the two words just read, which word comes next. It asked that question of a table of counts. Asking it of a network is what this section does, and the rows of section 2 are among the weights that move as a result.

  • surprise on the nine chapters—
  • surprise on chapter ten—
  • as unsure as choosing among—

Nothing has been trained yet. Training runs 47,782 positions six times over and takes a few seconds.

The network is the one from lesson 4 with its ends widened. Two words come in, each as 600 slots with a single one; each selects its sixteen-number row; the 32 numbers that result feed 32 sigmoid hidden units; those feed 600 output scores, one per word the network could say. The softmax turns the scores into probabilities. Then the true next word is revealed, the surprise is measured, and backpropagation walks the blame back through every weight it passed on the way forward — including the two selected rows, which are the only part of the table that moves for this position.

That last clause is the whole mechanism. A word's row is nudged only when the word appears, and it is nudged towards whatever makes the words that actually followed more likely. Two words that are followed by similar things receive similar nudges, thousands of times over, and drift towards similar rows. Nothing compares one row with another at any point, which is what lesson 8's table could never do — but here the comparison does not need to happen, because a shared consequence is enough.

There are 30,456 weights in total: 9,600 of them in the table of word rows, the remaining 20,856 in the two layers above it. The training positions are the 47,782 places in chapters one to nine where all three words — the two of context and the one to predict — are among the 600. Everything below is computed while you watch; nothing on this page was trained in advance.

Watch the two lines separate. The brown line keeps falling because the network is still improving on text it has already read; the blue line flattens, because that is where improvement has to be earned rather than memorised. Training longer moves the two apart rather than bringing both down.

Takeaway: Nothing compares two words, but words followed by similar things get similar corrections, so their rows drift together.

4

Reading the table afterwards

The rows are sixteen numbers each and nobody assigned them a meaning, so the only way to find out what they hold is to compare them. Beside each learned list is the counted list from lesson 8's method, run over these same 600 words and the same nine chapters, so the two differ in how they were arrived at and in nothing else.

learned, by training

    counted, as lesson 8

      Train the network in section 3 first; until then the learned column is reading untrained random numbers.

      With the default seed, “said” comes back beside “answered”, “asked”, “added” and “shouted”; “he” beside “k” and “she”; “lawyer” beside “painter” and “priest”. None of that was counted, and none of it was told to the network. It arrived because those words are followed by similar things, so their rows were pushed the same way.

      The two columns disagree more than they agree, and the disagreements are the informative part. Read a few words and a pattern shows: the learned rows do well on words that share a grammatical job — verbs of speech, pronouns, quantifiers — and less well on concrete nouns, where the counted profiles are often the more sensible of the two. “hand” is the plain case. Both methods put “hands” near the top, but counting follows it with “arm”, “head” and “coat”, while the learned row admits “evening” and “words” into the same four. “door” goes the other way and comes out well in both. With 82,000 words of one novel to learn from, the counted method is not the one that comes off worse.

      The similarity numbers themselves are not comparable between the columns. A counted profile is 2,400 slots that are mostly zero, so two profiles overlap in few places and the scores stay near 0.3; a learned row is sixteen numbers all in use, so scores near 0.9 are ordinary. Compare the orderings, not the values.

      One further difference is easy to miss. A counted profile is built from a word's own occurrences and can be computed for that word alone. A learned row exists only as part of a network trained on everything at once, and moving one row changes what every other row has to do. They are the same shape of object, arrived at by different means, and only one of them can be improved by a question it has not been asked yet.

      Takeaway: Learned rows group words by the job they do; on 82,000 words of one novel they do not beat counted profiles on concrete nouns.

      Before you try: Lesson 7's table had no row for a fifth of chapter ten's positions. What should a network do at those positions, and how well?

      5

      What a function buys over a table

      Lesson 7 ended on the table's silence and lesson 8 narrowed it by borrowing from neighbours. A network cannot be silent: whatever two words go in, 600 probabilities come out. The question this section settles by measurement is whether the answers it gives where the table had nothing are worth anything.

      Train the network in section 3 first; a walk needs a trained model to report anything.

      The walk is restricted, and the restriction has to be stated before the numbers are. Chapter ten gives 2,085 positions. The network can only be asked about the 1,102 of them whose three words are all among its 600; the other 983 involve a word it has no slot for, and section 6 is about those. Both models are scored on the same 1,102 positions, so the comparison is fair even though it covers about half the chapter.

      With the default seed the table has a row for 873 of the 1,102 and gets 150 of them right, which is 17.2 per cent of the positions it answers and 13.6 per cent of all of them. The network gets 148 of those same 873, so on the table's own ground the two are level and the table is very slightly ahead. On the 229 positions where the table has nothing, the network gets 39 — a rate of 17.0 per cent, the same rate it manages on contexts it has seen.

      That equality is the result worth having. The network is not better at recall; the table memorised those contexts and memorising is hard to beat. What the network has is a rate that does not depend on whether the exact context was in the training text, which is what it means for something to be a function of the words rather than a list of them. Lesson 8 bought the same coverage by borrowing counts from neighbours; here the coverage is not bought at all, because there was never a row to be missing.

      Two cautions on these figures. The sample is 1,102 positions, so a few percentage points is noise rather than signal. And the run depends on the seed: across the first few seeds the network's overall share of the 1,102 ranges from about 15 to about 17 per cent, which is enough to change whether it edges ahead of the table on the table's own rows. What does not move across seeds is the shape — full coverage, and much the same accuracy on seen and unseen contexts.

      Takeaway: The network answers every position at about one rate, whether or not the exact context was in the training text; the table answers four in five and never the rest.

      Before you try: The novel uses “pillow” three times. Which of the three models in lessons 7, 8 and 9 can say anything at all about it?

      6

      Where the slot runs out

      The silence of lesson 7 has moved but it has not gone, and section 1 said where it went. A one-hot vector has a slot for each word in a fixed vocabulary and no way to represent anything else, so the boundary of what this network can even be shown was drawn before training began.

      • sightings in chapters one to nine—
      • slot in the network's input—
      • row in the table—
      • can be predicted as a next word—

      Reports whether the word is inside the vocabulary, and what follows from the answer.

      Take the coverage first. The novel uses 5,082 distinct words and this network holds 600 of them, the rarest being “middle” at sixteen sightings. Those 600 account for 84 per cent of the words in chapters one to nine, which is why so much can be trained on so few. In chapter ten, 403 of the 2,087 words — 326 distinct ones — fall outside the vocabulary. For each of them there is no input slot, so the word cannot be read; and no output slot, so it cannot be said. This is a harder silence than lesson 7's. A count table with no row for a context can still be given a new row when it meets one. Adding a word here means adding a column to the input, a row to the table and a unit to the output, and then training again.

      Next, what the rows record. They were learned from one novel, so they carry that novel's usage in exactly the way lesson 8's profiles did. The proper names sit among the pronouns because the book is about a small cast, and the words this text happens to use interchangeably will look interchangeable here whether or not they are so in English. Nothing on this page knows English. It knows the first nine chapters of one translation of one book.

      Last, the honest scale note. The parts of this lesson that survive into a modern language model are the input convention and the learned table: words still enter as slots in a fixed vocabulary — usually pieces of words rather than whole ones — and the first weight matrix is still a table of vectors filled in by gradient descent. What does not survive is the rest of the architecture. A network of 30,456 weights reading two words of context is not a small version of a current model; it is the ancestor that showed the table could be learned at all. The distance between the two is measured in the amount of text, the length of context and a different arrangement of layers, and not one of those three is a detail.

      Yoshua Bengio, Réjean Ducharme, Pascal Vincent and Christian Jauvin, “A Neural Probabilistic Language Model,” Journal of Machine Learning Research 3 (2003), is the architecture on this page: a learned feature vector per word, a hidden layer over the concatenated vectors of the context, and a softmax over the vocabulary. Their argument was that learning the vectors and the predictor together would fight the sparseness that defeats a count table, and they measured it on corpora several hundred times the size of this one.

      Takeaway: A fixed vocabulary decides in advance what can be read or said, and no amount of training reaches a word without a slot.
