lesson 5 · convolution

Looking for the same thing everywhere

Lesson 2 ended with a complaint: no setting of 144 weights says pointed ears, wherever they are. A weight belongs to a pixel position, so the evidence it carries is evidence about that position, and moving the animal two pixels took the picture off the template. This lesson builds the standard repair. It has two parts, and the famous one turns out not to be the part that does the work.

Move the animal. The bright spot in the map moves with it.

Before you try: A detector that reads nine pixels can be shown any nine pixels in the picture. What would you want it to look for?

1

A detector that reads nine pixels

Instead of one weight per pixel across the whole picture, take nine weights arranged in a three-by-three square. Lay that square over some part of the picture, multiply each weight by the brightness underneath it, and add the nine products up. The result is a single number: how strongly this arrangement of nine weights answers to what is under it right now. The square is called a filter, and the sum is the same sum as every other lesson — w₁x₁ + ⋯ + w₉x₉ + b — with nine terms instead of 144 or two.

One point about the drawing before going on. A pixel's value is its brightness, and these pages draw a brighter pixel with more ink, exactly as lesson 2 does, so the animal is the dark shape on the page. A filter named for what is bright is named for where the numbers are high, which is where the page looks darkest.

These are the same nine weights wherever you put the box. That is the whole of the idea so far: the detector is a small thing that can be asked about any part of the picture, rather than a large thing tied to all of it. Nothing has been learned yet — these four filters are set by hand, as the weights in lesson 3 were, and section 4 throws them away and finds its own.

Takeaway: A filter is a small set of weights that can be applied to any position in the picture.

Before you try: If the picture moves two pixels to the right, what happens to the map of responses?

2

Nine weights, a hundred positions

Now stop choosing a position and use every one. A three-by-three box fits into a twelve-by-twelve picture in 100 places, so the filter can be asked 100 questions and give 100 answers. Arrange those answers in the same layout as the positions they came from and you have a picture of your own: a map of where in the image this detector found what it was looking for. This is a convolution, and the map it produces is called a feature map.

Applying the filter at 100 positions does not cost 100 filters. There is one set of nine numbers and it is reused at every position, which is what weight sharing means. The saving is real, but it is not the point, and section 5 measures what it is worth on its own. The interesting property is on the screen: the map moved with the picture. A template cannot do that, because a template has no map — it collapses everything to one number immediately.

Takeaway: One filter applied everywhere turns a picture into a map of where it responded, and that map shifts when the picture does.

Before you try: To answer is there a pointed ear anywhere in this picture, which do you need: the map, or one number taken from it?

3

Keeping that it fired, not where

The map still has a position for every answer, so a layer that reads the map position by position is back to caring where things are. The repair is to read it in a way that cannot tell. Take the largest value in the whole map and keep only that. The result says how strongly this detector responded anywhere in the picture, and says nothing whatever about where. This is pooling, and it is the step that buys the property lesson 2 lacked.

The position runs through its whole range as you move the slider and the value barely moves. That is the property being manufactured, and it is manufactured by throwing information away: after the maximum is taken, nothing downstream can recover where the detector fired, or whether it fired twice. Section 6 returns to what that costs.

Kunihiko Fukushima, “Neocognitron: A Self-organizing Neural Network Model for a Mechanism of Pattern Recognition Unaffected by Shift in Position,” Biological Cybernetics 36 (1980), separates the two jobs into two kinds of cell: S-cells that apply the same detector at many positions, and C-cells above them that tolerate a shift in where the S-cells responded. The title names the property this section is after, and the architecture keeps the two ingredients apart, which is the distinction section 5 measures.

Takeaway: Taking the largest response discards where the detector fired, which is what makes the answer survive a shift.

Before you try: The four filters start as small random numbers. What should they end up looking for, given that the ears are the only thing telling these animals apart?

4

Filling the filters in

The four filters in the previous sections were set by hand. They do not have to be. A filter is a short list of weights, the maximum is a function of those weights, and the verdict is a function of the maximum, so there is a chain from the error back to every one of the nine numbers — which is exactly what lesson 4 built. Backpropagation runs through a shared filter the same way it runs through anything else, with one difference: a weight used at 100 positions collects blame from all 100, so the corrections are added up before the weight moves.

Watch the four filters as the error falls: they do not arrive at anything as tidy as the hand-set ones, and several runs produce filters that look like nothing in particular while still scoring well. A filter is not required to be interpretable, and reading one as though it detects a namable thing is a guess about a picture, not a fact about the weights.

Yann LeCun, Bernhard Boser, John Denker, Donnie Henderson, Richard Howard, Wayne Hubbard and Lawrence Jackel, “Backpropagation Applied to Handwritten Zip Code Recognition,” Neural Computation 1 (1989), trained shared filters by backpropagation on real handwritten digits, which is the combination this section runs: Fukushima's architecture with lesson 4's learning rule. Their network read 16×16 digits and was trained on thousands of them, not sixteen.

Takeaway: A shared filter is trained like any other weight, with the corrections from every position it was used at added together.

Before you try: Two ingredients went into this: sharing one filter across all positions, and discarding where it fired. Which of the two do you think repairs lesson 2's failure?

5

The nudge from lesson 2

Lesson 2 broke its classifier by moving the animal two pixels. Here is the same nudge, applied to three machines trained on the same 16 pictures and tested on 80 they never saw. The first is lesson 2's template. The second is this lesson's network. The third is the one that settles the question: it has the shared filters and the feature maps, and it reads those maps position by position instead of taking the maximum. It is convolution with the pooling left out.

Read the last column against the first. The machine with shared filters and no pooling has all the machinery this lesson is named for, and at a two-pixel shift it scores what lesson 2's template scores. Sharing the weights saved a great many parameters and bought almost no tolerance to movement. What bought the tolerance was the maximum — the step that makes the answer unable to depend on position, because it has discarded position.

This is one experiment on one kind of picture, and a single run is not the finding. Over twelve starting seeds the pooled network averaged 0.907 at a two-pixel shift, with runs between 0.823 and 0.944; the same network without pooling averaged 0.611, with runs between 0.525 and 0.692; lesson 2's template, which has no random start to vary, scores 0.610. The two ranges do not overlap at any seed, and the unpooled network's average finishes 0.001 above the template it was supposed to improve on.

Takeaway: Weight sharing saves parameters; discarding position is what survives the shift. They are separate ideas and only one of them repairs lesson 2.

Before you try: The network is now indifferent to where the animal is. Name a question about these pictures it has therefore made itself unable to answer.

6

What it does not give you

Shifting is one transformation out of many, and the previous section bought tolerance to exactly that one and no other. Everything lesson 2 did to its classifier can be done here, and most of it still works.

score on 80 held-out pictures —

Train the network in section 4 first.

Mirroring and inverting are not shifts, and nothing in this architecture was built to absorb them. Inverting is the worst of the three for the same reason it was in lesson 2: it flips the sign of every contribution, so a machine that was reliably right becomes reliably wrong. A convolutional network is not generally tolerant of change; it is tolerant of the one transformation that pooling was arranged to discard.

Then the cost of the repair itself. The maximum keeps whether a detector fired and drops where, so any question whose answer depends on where has been made unanswerable by construction. Are the ears above the eyes or beside them is such a question, and it happens to be the distinction these two classes are built on. This network gets that right because each filter looks at a patch big enough to contain the answer locally, not because the architecture preserved the arrangement. A single pooled layer taking the maximum over the entire picture is the most extreme version of the trade; real networks pool over small windows and stack several layers, keeping coarse position while discarding fine position, which is a compromise this page does not demonstrate.

Last, the scale note. Four filters of nine weights on a twelve-by-twelve grid, trained on sixteen generated drawings, is not a small version of a modern vision model. The pictures are drawings this page generated, with the ears carrying all the information by construction, so a high score here is evidence about these drawings and not about photographs. What carries over is the pair of ideas and the order of their importance, which is what the previous section measured.

Takeaway: Tolerance to shifting was bought deliberately, applies to shifting alone, and is paid for by discarding the position it discards.
