lesson 8 · word vectors

Words as points

The last lesson ended in silence: a table of counts met a pair of words it had never seen and had nothing to say — no guess, no near miss, no measure of how far off it was. This lesson builds the missing notion of a near miss from the same nine chapters of Kafka's The Trial. Every word is given a profile of the company it keeps; a profile is a list of numbers, a list of numbers is a point, and two points can be near or far. That distance is what the table lacked, and the last two sections measure what it buys.

word profiles built 0

Type any word the novel uses at least five times, and its nearest neighbours are found by comparing profiles.

A short guide to the numbers

Everything on this page starts from counts: how often one word stands within two places of another in a fixed text. A count becomes a surprise by comparing it with chance: the logarithm of how often the company was seen, divided by how often random shuffling would produce it. Meeting “the” is never surprising, so it scores near zero however often it happens; company seen less than chance predicts is dropped. The expected rate of very rare company is nudged upward before the comparison, so one lucky meeting cannot dominate a profile.

A word's surprises, one slot per possible companion per position, form its profile — a list of 5,796 numbers, scaled so the list has length one. The similarity of two words is the angle between their profiles, measured as a cosine: multiply matching slots and add. Identical company gives 1; no shared company gives 0.

The map flattens each profile onto two fixed directions, found by repeatedly averaging the profiles when the page loads. It is a shadow of the full 5,796-number space, and distances in a shadow are approximate.

Before you try: “lawyer” is followed by 96 different words in this novel, “painter” by 77. How many of those do you expect them to share?

1

Two rows the table cannot compare

The table from the last lesson holds a row for every word it has seen: everything that ever followed it, with counts. Put two of those rows side by side and read them. You can see at once that they resemble each other — and you can see a catch in that resemblance, which the next section resolves. The table sees neither, permanently, because nothing in it ever compares one row with another.

      “lawyer” occurs 190 times in chapters one to nine and “painter” 125, and their rows overlap: 30 of the 96 distinct words ever seen after “lawyer” also follow “painter”. Now the catch. Read what the shared entries are — “and”, “was”, “had” — words that follow almost anything, so that a raw overlap flatters every pair. The raw material of similarity is in the counts, tangled up with company that means nothing; separating the two is the next section's job. What the table itself does with any of it is simpler to state: nothing. Its only operation is looking a row up. Every word is an island, and a pair of words it never met is a place it has never been — which is exactly where the last lesson left it, silent.

      One difference in bookkeeping before going on. Here “Lawyer,” and “lawyer” are the same word: capitals and surrounding punctuation are stripped. The last lesson kept them apart to stay faithful to the exact text it was quoting, and counted 8,671 distinct words; this lesson is after the words themselves, and counts 5,082.

      Takeaway: The counts already contain the raw material of similarity — overlapping company — but the table has no operation that reads it.

      Before you try: Which pair should keep more similar company — “door” and “window”, or “door” and “slowly”?

      2

      A word is its company

      Here is the whole method. For each of the 1,449 words the novel uses at least five times, walk chapters one to nine and count what stands near it: the word two places before, one before, one after, two after, each position kept separate. Every word ends up with a profile of 5,796 slots — 1,449 possible companions in each of four positions. “lawyer” fills 271 of them; the rest stay zero.

       

         

          Each column lists the profile's heaviest slots: the companion, the position it was seen in, and its weight. Shaded slots are filled in both profiles.

           

          surprise = log(seen ÷ expected by chance)

          Raw counts would be ruled by “the” and “and”, which stand near everything and tell you nothing. So each count is turned into a surprise: how much more often the company was seen than chance alone would put it there. Meeting “the” scores near zero however often it happens. Meeting “huld” two places before — the lawyer's own name — scores high. Company seen less often than chance is dropped rather than held against the word.

          The idea that a word is characterised by the distribution of its company is much older than the machinery, and is called the distributional hypothesis. The profile makes it mechanical: a list of 5,796 numbers is a word vector, which is to say a point in a space with 5,796 directions. Two points can be compared. The measure used here is cosine similarity — the angle between the two profiles — which reaches 1 for identical company and 0 for none shared. “lawyer” against “painter” scores 0.22, closer than “lawyer” comes to any other word in the novel; against “slowly” it scores 0.05. The table's rows could never do this. The vectors do nothing else.

          J. R. Firth, “A Synopsis of Linguistic Theory 1930–1955,” in Studies in Linguistic Analysis (Oxford: Blackwell, 1957): “You shall know a word by the company it keeps.” Firth was arguing about meaning and context between linguists, decades before anyone counted company into vectors; the sentence became the motto of the counting method all the same.

          Takeaway: Counting and reweighting a word's company turns it into a vector, and vectors can be compared — the comparison recovers a likeness nobody labelled.

          Before you try: The map is drawn from company alone. What do you expect to gather around “said”?

          3

          The vocabulary, drawn as a map

          A point with 5,796 coordinates cannot be drawn. What can be drawn is a shadow: every profile flattened onto two fixed directions along which the profiles spread widely, computed when this page loaded. Each dot below is one of the 1,449 words. Look one up and its nearest neighbours light up around it.

           

          The regions have names nobody gave them. The people of the novel — lawyer, painter, businessman, priest — stand together; the ways of saying — said, asked, answered, shouted — keep to their own district; door, window, bed and table sit side by side; two, three and several make a small numeric family. Every one of these groupings is a regularity in how the words are used across nine chapters, surfaced by counting and nothing else.

          Read the picture with care, in two respects. The two drawn directions were chosen by the author from among the strongest few because they spread these families visibly; a different pair gives a different picture, though the same words stay together. And a shadow compresses: words can land near each other on the map while their profiles are far apart. The neighbour list is computed in the full space — when the map and the list disagree, believe the list.

          Scott Deerwester, Susan Dumais, George Furnas, Thomas Landauer and Richard Harshman, “Indexing by Latent Semantic Analysis,” Journal of the American Society for Information Science 41, no. 6 (1990). Compressing a word-by-context count table with this matrix arithmetic was introduced for searching document collections; the authors presented the recovered directions as useful for retrieval rather than as meanings.

          Takeaway: Similar company becomes literal nearness, and the vocabulary arranges itself into families — people, rooms, ways of saying — that were never labelled.

          Before you try: The exact table answers about three chapter-ten positions in five. With neighbours allowed to vote, how many should it answer — and should those answers be as accurate?

          4

          Borrowed counts

          The held-out measurement from the last lesson can now be rerun with one addition. Walking chapter ten, the model asks at each position whether it has a row for the last two words. Where it does, it answers from the row, as before. Where it does not, the forty nearest neighbours of each of the two words vote: every neighbour that completes a known pair offers that row's counts, weighted by similarity, and the largest total wins. “about nine” has no row in the table; its neighbours reach the row for “about eleven”, and the vote answers “o'clock” — which is correct.

          Walks the 81,559 two-word positions of chapters one to nine once, and the 2,085 of chapter ten twice — with exact rows only, then letting neighbours vote wherever the table is silent.

          The exact table answers 60 per cent of chapter ten's positions; 13 per cent of those answers name the next word. With the vote it answers 84 per cent. The 503 borrowed answers are exactly right one time in twelve — worse than the rows the table owns, better than the silence they replace — and the misses change character: where there was nothing, there is now usually a word of the right kind. This is generalisation, bought with similarity, and it is modest. On the chapters it memorised the exact table is right 56 per cent of the time, and no amount of borrowing brings chapter ten anywhere near that.

          Yoshua Bengio, Réjean Ducharme, Pascal Vincent and Christian Jauvin, “A Neural Probabilistic Language Model,” Journal of Machine Learning Research 3 (2003). The influential case for giving words learned feature vectors so that similar words share what the model learns about them. The vectors there were trained jointly with the next-word prediction itself; the ones on this page are only counted, which is the smaller half of that idea.

          Pairs the table knows are continued from exact rows. Where it is silent, the neighbours vote; words the vote contributed are shaded blue.

          This is the box from the end of the last lesson, refitted. Type a pair the novel never contains and it usually no longer trades your words away for the ability to say anything at all — the neighbours vote, and the model keeps talking about what you said. The old failure is still underneath, in two layers. When no neighbour completes a known pair, the model falls back to your last word alone, dropping context exactly as before; the message under the box says so when it happens. And when your last word is one the novel never uses, the answer is still nothing, because a word with no profile has no neighbours to ask. That silence has not moved. It is the subject of the last section.

          Takeaway: Borrowed counts replace silence with near misses — the model answers far more often, is right slightly more often, and stays far below its own memorised chapters.

          Before you try: The novel uses “pillow” three times and “computer” never. What should the profiles be able to say about each?

          5

          Where counting ends

          Everything on this page rests on counted company, so it ends where counting ends. This section is the honest inventory: which words got no profile at all, and what the profiles that do exist actually record.

          • sightings in chapters one to nine—
          • profile slots filled—
          • nearest neighbour—

            Reports what the model holds for the word: its count, its profile, and its neighbours — or the honest nothing.

            First the coverage. Chapter ten contains 97 occurrences of words — 84 distinct ones — that the first nine chapters never use, and another 124 occurrences of words seen fewer than five times, too few for a profile. For all of these, this lesson adds nothing: no vector, no neighbours, no vote. Similarity widened the table's reach; it gave the model no way to meet a genuinely new word.

            Then the neighbours that do exist, read for what they are. “door” and “mouth” score 0.22 — as high as “lawyer” and “painter” — because both get opened, shut and stood in front of. Shared company is not shared meaning. And “bank” sits among “hallway” and “corner”, because the only bank in this novel is the office building where K. works: a profile counted from one book records that book's usage, and nothing else. These vectors do not know English. They know The Trial.

            Last, the vectors on this page were counted once and then fixed, and every word owns a single point no matter which of its uses is on the page. Today's language models keep this lesson's geometry and change where it comes from: their word vectors are parameters, adjusted by the machinery of lessons 1 to 4 until predicting the next word comes out better, and reconsidered in context each time a sentence is read. What carries over from this page unchanged is the idea itself — that a word can be a point, and that nearness between points can do work a table of separate rows cannot. The next lesson makes exactly that change on this same novel: it hands the next-word question to a network, lets backpropagation fill in a table of word vectors instead of counting one, and measures which of the two comes out better.

            Takeaway: Profiles end where counting ends — rare words stay islands, neighbours share company rather than meaning, and similarity learned from one novel is that novel's.
