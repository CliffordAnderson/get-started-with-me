lesson 1 · perceptrons

A machine that draws one line

A perceptron takes a few numbers, weighs them up, and answers yes or no. Everything it can ever decide is a straight boundary through the space of its inputs — and it can find that boundary from labelled examples alone, by correcting its own mistakes.

learning

Watching the rule correct itself, one example at a time.

1

What it computes

Give the unit a list of numbers. It multiplies each by a weight of its own, adds them together, adds one more number that belongs to no input — the bias — and fires if the total is at least zero. That is the whole machine. A weight says how much an input counts and in which direction; the bias says how much evidence is needed before the unit will fire at all.

y = step(w·x + b)

Set w₂ to zero and the second input stops mattering, whatever it is. Make it negative and that input now argues against firing. Nothing else in the unit can express a preference.

Notice how little the bias cares about the input. It shifts every total by the same amount, whatever came in, which is why it is the unit's threshold rather than one of its opinions.

2

Where the line comes from

Plot every possible input as a point. The inputs where the total comes to exactly zero form a straight line, and that line is the unit's decision: fire on one side, stay quiet on the other. The two weights are a direction — the arrow below points square across the line, toward the firing side — and the bias slides the line along that direction without turning it.

1.00 0.80 −0.85

at (0.35, 0.65): w·x + b = 0.02

Multiply all three numbers by ten and the line does not budge — the same points satisfy the same equation. Only the totals grow. The unit's answer depends on the sign of the total, never on its size, which is exactly why the step at zero throws away information a later lesson will want back.

3

Finding the line from examples

Nobody sets the weights by hand. Show the unit a labelled example; if it answers correctly, change nothing. If it answers 0 when the answer was 1, add that example's coordinates to the weights, which tips the line toward it. If it answers 1 when the answer was 0, subtract them. Repeat through the examples until a whole pass goes by without a mistake.

4

What the rule guarantees, and what it doesn't

If some line separates the two classes, this rule is guaranteed to find one, in a finite number of corrections. The count depends on how much room there is between the classes: a wide gap is found quickly, a narrow one slowly. Rosenblatt proved the convergence; Novikoff's 1962 bound made the dependence on that gap precise.

What the rule does not do is tell you which situation you are in. Run it on classes that no line can separate and it behaves exactly the same way: correcting, overshooting, correcting back, forever. There is no signal in the procedure that distinguishes not yet from never. That gap between a model that cannot express something and an optimizer that keeps trying is worth holding onto; it recurs at every scale of this subject.

The other thing to keep separate is the unit from the machine. What we have been operating is one threshold unit. Rosenblatt's perceptron was a larger arrangement: a grid of input cells, a middle bank of feature detectors wired to them at random and never adjusted, and a final layer of threshold units whose weights were the only things that learned.

That distinction is what the next lesson turns on. A single unit reaches its limit at a function small enough to draw on the back of an envelope.

Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts, “A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity” (1943), gave the threshold unit. Frank Rosenblatt, “The Perceptron: A Probabilistic Model for Information Storage and Organization in the Brain,” Psychological Review 65 (1958), added the learning rule.
