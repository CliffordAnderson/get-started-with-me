About

What these lessons are, how to read one, and how the site was made.

What these are

Interactive lessons on artificial intelligence. Every diagram is computed in your browser from the numbers on screen: move a slider and the mathematics runs again. Nothing is a recording, so when a demonstration fails, it fails for the real reason, and you can go looking for it.

The early lessons need only multiplying and adding; later ones introduce slopes and gradients, with help alongside. Each is as clear about what its machine cannot do as about what it can — the first ends by setting out what its learning rule does not guarantee, and the second finishes by breaking the classifier it has just trained.

A lesson takes ten to fifteen minutes to read, plus time for its experiments. They build on each other, but nothing stops you starting anywhere.

How to read one

Each lesson is a few short panels with a rail across the top. Nothing to install, no account. The layouts work on a phone, and the interactive diagrams have keyboard alternatives. Use “Show all sections” for continuous reading, or print to include every section.

Technical words open a definition in place, so looking one up leaves your experiment as it was. The full glossary explains each term plainly before pointing at Wikipedia for the long version.

The examples are stored nowhere. They are generated in your browser from a seeded random number generator each time a lesson runs, which is what keeps a held-out set genuinely held out.

How it was made

In conversation with an AI — Claude, working directly in the repository, alongside a person who set the brief, argued with the drafts and decided what shipped. The commit history names the model as a co-author on the work it did, and it seems fair to say so where a reader will see it. Which sentence came from which is not a line that can be drawn any more: the prose went back and forth until it read in one voice, and so did the code.

The risk of working this way is a confident sentence about a number nobody measured. So no figure enters the prose until the shipped code has been run and made to produce it. That rule caught several drafted claims: the error surface in the fourth lesson oscillates where a draft had it diverging, and the pair of cases a failed training run collapses together turned out to vary from run to run. What is left standing is what the measurement supported.

Who wrote them

Written by Clifford Anderson, with Claude. The source is on GitHub, where the commits record how each lesson came together and what had to be corrected on the way.

If a lesson states something wrong, or a demonstration behaves differently on your machine than the prose says it should, open an issue on the repository. A site that calls its figures measured rather than asserted has to be willing to hear when one of them is not.

Every plot on every lesson is computed live in your browser; no server does any of the arithmetic. Back to the lessons.
