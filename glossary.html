Glossary

Every technical word the lessons use. Each entry says what the term means in plain language first, then what it means exactly, and points at Wikipedia for the long version.

activation function

The last thing a unit does before it reports an answer. It takes the single number the unit has worked out by adding up its inputs and turns it into the output — squashing it into a yes or no, or into a confidence somewhere between the two.

Formally, the function applied to the weighted sum. Which one you choose decides whether the unit can be trained by gradient methods at all: a step has no usable slope, a sigmoid does.

Used in lessons 3 and 4Wikipedia

ADALINE

A machine built in 1960 that learned in a different way from the perceptron: instead of counting how many answers it got wrong, it measured how far off it was and slid its weights a little way down that measurement.

Widrow and Hoff's adaptive linear element, trained by the delta rule. It is the perceptron's exact contemporary and the direct ancestor of gradient descent as it is used today, though it handled only a single unit.

Used in lesson 4Wikipedia

artificial neuron

The smallest working part of a neural network. It takes several numbers in, multiplies each by its own weight, adds them all up along with a bias, and puts the total through an activation function to produce one number out.

A mathematical caricature of a nerve cell, first written down by McCulloch and Pitts in 1943. It borrows the idea of summed inputs and a firing threshold and almost nothing else.

Used in lessons 1 to 4Wikipedia

backpropagation

The method that works out how much each weight in a layered network is to blame for the network's mistake. The output's error is known directly; every weight further back gets its share by passing that error backwards along the same connections that carried the answer forwards.

Repeated application of the chain rule to compute the gradient of the error with respect to every parameter. Published for neural networks by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams in 1986, though the same computation had been described by Linnainmaa in 1970 and by Werbos in 1974.

Used in lessons 3 and 4Wikipedia

backward chaining

Working from the question towards the evidence. Rather than grinding through everything that could be concluded from what is known, the machine starts with the goal it was set, finds the rules that would establish it, and takes each of their premises as a new goal — asking only when it reaches something no rule concludes.

Goal-directed inference over a rule base: a depth-first search backwards through the rules, in which an unestablished premise becomes a subgoal and the leaves of the search are questions put to the user. Its complement is forward chaining. It is what makes a consultation feel like an interrogation, and what keeps the number of questions small.

Used in lesson 5Wikipedia

bias

A number added to the total regardless of what the inputs are. It sets how eager the unit is to say yes: a large positive bias makes it fire on the slightest encouragement, a large negative one makes it hard to convince.

The constant term in the weighted sum. Geometrically it slides the decision boundary without rotating it; without one, every boundary would be forced through the origin.

Used in lessons 1 to 4Wikipedia

brittleness

The habit of working well inside the cases a system was built for and failing without warning just outside them. A brittle system does not grow gradually less reliable as you leave familiar ground; it carries on answering in the same confident tone while being wrong.

The characteristic failure of symbolic rule-based systems, whose competence stops exactly where their rules stop and which hold no representation of their own boundary. Learned models have a softer version of the same problem, degrading away from the data they were trained on, though usually with some sag in confidence to go with it. Wikipedia’s article on software brittleness describes a different phenomenon, the decay of code under modification.

Used in lesson 5no Wikipedia article

certainty factor

A number between −1 and +1 attached to a rule or a conclusion, saying how strongly the evidence supports it: +1 settled truth, −1 settled falsity, 0 no evidence either way. Two rules reaching the same conclusion have their numbers combined, so agreement accumulates without ever quite arriving at certainty.

MYCIN’s scheme for inexact reasoning, set out by Shortliffe and Buchanan in 1975. A certainty factor measures how far evidence shifts belief rather than how probable something is, and the combining rules — a + b − ab for two positives, a normalised sum where they disagree — coincide with probability theory only under conditions that are usually not met. It was adopted because eliciting a joint distribution from an expert was impossible and a number attached to a single rule was not.

Used in lesson 5Wikipedia

chain rule

The rule for working out how much a change at the start of a chain of steps affects the end of it. If turning a handle twice as fast makes a wheel spin twice as fast, and that wheel turning twice as fast makes a belt move three times as fast, the handle affects the belt six times over.

The rule of calculus for differentiating a composition of functions: the derivatives of the steps multiply. It is the whole mechanism behind backpropagation.

Used in lesson 4Wikipedia

decision boundary

The dividing line between the inputs a machine answers yes to and the ones it answers no to. Stand on the boundary itself and the machine is exactly undecided.

The set of points where the output changes class. For a single unit it is a straight line in two dimensions, a plane in three, and a hyperplane in general; a hidden layer lets it bend.

Used in lessons 1 to 4Wikipedia

delta rule

A recipe for adjusting a weight: look at how far the answer was from the target, multiply that by the input the weight was attached to, and nudge the weight by a small amount in that direction.

The least-mean-squares rule of Widrow and Hoff, equivalent to gradient descent on the squared error for a single unit. Backpropagation is what it becomes once there is a hidden layer in the way.

Used in lesson 4Wikipedia

epoch

One complete pass through all the training examples. Saying a network trained for three hundred epochs means it went through the whole set of examples three hundred times over.

A unit of training time rather than of computation, so the same epoch count means very different amounts of work on different sets. Wikipedia has no article on the term; it is defined in passing in most treatments of training.

Used in lesson 4no Wikipedia article

error surface

A landscape you get by imagining every possible setting of the weights spread out on the ground, with the height at each spot being how wrong the machine would be if it used those weights. Learning is walking downhill on it.

The graph of the loss as a function of the parameters. For one unit with a smooth activation it has a single valley; add a hidden layer and it acquires local minima and long flat regions.

Used in lesson 4Wikipedia

expert system

A program that reaches conclusions in one narrow professional domain by applying a body of rules gathered from human specialists. What it knows is held as sentences rather than as numbers, applied by a general-purpose interpreter, and it can usually show which sentences produced a given answer.

The dominant applied form of symbolic AI from the middle 1970s into the 1980s, conventionally divided into a rule base and an inference engine so that what the system knows can be replaced without touching the machinery that reasons. MYCIN is the canonical example.

Used in lesson 5Wikipedia

feature

One of the individual measurements you hand the machine about a thing. If the thing is a small picture, one feature might be the brightness of a single pixel; if it is a person, it might be their height.

A component of the input vector. Choosing what the features should be was for decades most of the work; what changed with layered networks is that a network can compose its own from raw measurements.

Used in lessons 1 and 2Wikipedia

forward chaining

Working from what is known towards whatever follows from it. The machine takes the facts it has, fires every rule whose premise they satisfy, adds the conclusions to what it knows, and goes round again until nothing new appears.

Data-directed inference, the complement of backward chaining. It suits situations where facts arrive continuously and anything derivable is worth deriving. It is a poor fit for consultation, where most of what could be concluded is beside the point and every new fact costs somebody an answer.

Used in lesson 5Wikipedia

generalisation

Whether the machine gets things right on examples it has never seen. Getting the training examples right is easy and proves very little; the question that matters is what happens next.

Performance on the underlying distribution rather than on the training sample, estimated with a held-out set. The gap between the two is the generalisation error.

Used in lesson 2Wikipedia

gradient

Which way is uphill, and how steeply. At any spot on a landscape the gradient is an arrow pointing in the direction that climbs fastest, and its length says how sharp the climb is.

The vector of partial derivatives of a function with respect to each of its variables. On flat ground it is zero, which is why a step function gives a learning rule nothing to work with.

Used in lessons 3 and 4Wikipedia

gradient descent

Learning by repeatedly taking a small step downhill. Work out which way is down, move a little way in that direction, and do it again until the ground stops falling away.

Iteratively subtracting a multiple of the gradient from the parameters. The multiple is the learning rate; too large and the steps overshoot the valley instead of settling into it.

Used in lesson 4Wikipedia

half-plane

Everything on one side of a straight line. Draw a line across a page and the ink on the left of it is a half-plane.

The region on one side of a hyperplane. A single unit answers yes on exactly one half-plane of its input space, which is the whole of what it can express and the reason it cannot compute XOR.

Used in lessons 3 and 4Wikipedia

held-out set

Examples deliberately kept away from the machine while it learns, so they can be used afterwards as a fair test. If it has already seen the answer, the test tells you nothing.

A test set drawn from the same distribution as the training data but disjoint from it. In lesson 2 the two sets come from disjoint ranges of the generator seed, so the separation is genuine.

Used in lesson 2Wikipedia

hidden layer

A row of units sitting between the inputs and the output, whose answers nobody ever sees or specifies. They exist only to re-describe the input for the units that come after them.

Hidden because it appears in neither the input nor the output, so no training example says what it ought to produce. That is exactly the difficulty backpropagation solves.

Used in lessons 3 and 4Wikipedia

inference engine

The part of a rule-based system that does the reasoning, as distinct from the rules it reasons over. It chooses which rule to consider next, works out how to establish a premise and how to combine what it finds — and it knows nothing whatever about the subject matter.

The interpreter in an expert system. Its separation from the rule base is the design’s central claim: one engine serves any domain, and a domain is changed by editing sentences rather than code. EMYCIN — MYCIN with its medical rules taken out — was the first widely used demonstration.

Used in lesson 5Wikipedia

joint distribution

A statement of how likely every combination of several quantities is, taken together rather than one at a time. Knowing how common each finding is on its own does not tell you how often they turn up in company, and it is the company that usually matters.

The probability distribution over a set of variables considered as a whole. Correct reasoning under uncertainty in general requires it, which is what makes exact probabilistic inference expensive: the table grows exponentially with the number of variables. Certainty factors were an attempt to get by without one.

Used in lesson 5Wikipedia

knowledge acquisition

Getting what an expert knows out of the expert and into a form a machine can use. In practice this means long interviews, because people who decide cases fluently are often unable to state the rule they are following, and want to qualify whatever they do state.

The bottleneck of the expert system programme, and the reason a rule base is expensive to build and expensive to keep current. Edward Feigenbaum named it as the central obstacle. Obtaining the rules from data instead is what the rest of these lessons are about.

Used in lesson 5Wikipedia

knowledge engineering

The discipline of building a rule base: interviewing specialists, turning what they say into rules, testing the result against cases, and going back with the failures. The knowledge engineer is the person who does this, and is generally not the expert.

The practice built around knowledge acquisition and the design of expert systems. It treats what a system knows as something to be elicited and written down rather than inferred from examples, which is the whole of the distinction between this tradition and machine learning.

Used in lesson 5Wikipedia

label

The right answer attached to an example, put there by somebody who already knew it. A picture of a cat carries the label cat.

The target value the machine is asked to reproduce. Having labels is what makes a problem supervised.

Used in lessons 1 and 2Wikipedia

learning rate

How big a step to take each time you adjust the weights. Small steps are reliable but slow; large ones are quick until they start jumping clean over the thing they were aiming at.

The scalar multiplying the gradient in an update. It is chosen rather than learned, which makes it a hyperparameter.

Used in lesson 4Wikipedia

linear classifier

Any machine whose whole decision comes down to which side of a straight divide the input falls on. A perceptron is one.

A classifier whose decision boundary is a hyperplane, so its output depends on the input only through a weighted sum. Cheap, well understood, and unable to represent anything that is not linearly separable.

Used in lessons 1 to 3Wikipedia

linearly separable

Said of a set of examples that can be split correctly by a single straight line, with all the yeses on one side and all the noes on the other. Some sets can be; XOR's four cases cannot.

Two classes are linearly separable when some hyperplane puts them in opposite half-spaces. Rosenblatt's convergence theorem guarantees the perceptron rule finds such a boundary whenever one exists, and says nothing at all when one does not.

Used in lessons 1 to 3Wikipedia

local minimum

A dip in the landscape that is the lowest point nearby, but not the lowest point anywhere. Something that only ever walks downhill will stop there and stay, because every direction out is up.

A point where the gradient vanishes and the surrounding region is higher, without being the global minimum. Their existence is why training a layered network comes with no convergence guarantee.

Used in lesson 4Wikipedia

margin

How much clear space there is between the dividing line and the nearest example. A line that only just scrapes past the closest point is correct but precarious; one with room on both sides is the same answer held more confidently.

The distance from the decision boundary to the nearest training point. Where several boundaries score equally, preferring the widest margin is a reasonable way to break the tie.

Used in lessons 1 and 2Wikipedia

MYCIN

A program written at Stanford in the early 1970s to recommend antibiotic therapy for bacterial infections. It put questions to a physician, applied several hundred rules, and produced a recommendation with a certainty attached and a derivation available on request.

Edward Shortliffe’s doctoral work, supervised by Bruce Buchanan and Stanley Cohen, written in Lisp and developed over five or six years. In a blinded evaluation published in 1979 its meningitis recommendations were judged acceptable more often than those of the five faculty specialists it was compared against. It was never used on a patient. Counts of its rules differ by source and by year, the base having grown throughout; figures of roughly 450 and roughly 600 are both given.

Used in lesson 5Wikipedia

parity

Whether the number of switches that are on is odd or even. With two inputs it is the same question as XOR; with a hundred inputs it is the same question asked of a hundred switches.

The parity function is the deep case of the limit that XOR illustrates. Minsky and Papert showed its order equals the size of the whole input, so no perceptron whose parts each see only a limited region can compute it however the votes are weighted.

Used in lesson 3Wikipedia

perceptron

The machine these lessons start with. It takes a few numbers, multiplies each by a weight, adds a bias, and answers yes if the total clears zero — and it can find those weights for itself from labelled examples by correcting its own mistakes.

Rosenblatt's 1958 model, and the learning rule that goes with it. The rule provably converges whenever the examples are linearly separable. Note that Minsky and Papert use the word for a whole class of machines rather than for one unit.

Used in lessons 1 to 4Wikipedia

Perceptrons (1969)

The book by Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert that worked out precisely what machines of this kind can and cannot do. It is often blamed for stalling the field, which is rather more than it actually claimed.

Its real subjects are the order of predicates, parity and connectedness rather than XOR. It observes that layered machines are computationally universal, and doubts only that a learning theory as clean as the perceptron rule would extend to them — a judgement it invited its readers to refute.

Used in lessons 2 and 3Wikipedia

pixel

One square of a picture, holding a single brightness. A picture is a grid of them, and to a machine it arrives as a list of numbers rather than as a picture at all.

The smallest addressable element of a raster image. Lesson 2 uses a twelve by twelve grid, so each picture is a list of 144 numbers and the unit needs 144 weights, one per pixel.

Used in lesson 2Wikipedia

production rule

An IF/THEN sentence: a premise about the world, and something to conclude when the premise holds. A rule says nothing about when it ought to be applied or what else is true — that is the interpreter’s business — which is what allows rules to be added and removed one at a time.

The unit of knowledge in a production system. Modularity is the point and also the catch: rules are independent by construction, so nobody need hold the whole base in mind to write one, and nobody can easily foresee how a new rule will interact with the several hundred already there.

Used in lesson 5Wikipedia

rule base

The collection of rules a system reasons over — everything it knows, kept separate from the machinery that applies it. You can read a rule base, disagree with one line of it, and change that line without retraining anything.

The knowledge half of an expert system, divided from the inference engine. Legible one rule at a time and a good deal less so in bulk: the interactions between rules are written down nowhere, which is where brittleness and the cost of maintenance both come from.

Used in lesson 5Wikipedia

seeded generator

A source of numbers that look random but are worked out from a starting number, so the same starting number always produces the same sequence. It gives you randomness you can reproduce exactly.

A pseudorandom number generator. These lessons generate their examples from one rather than shipping a fixed data file, which is what lets a page draw as many fresh examples as it needs and keep a held-out set genuinely held out.

Used in lessons 2 and 4Wikipedia

sigmoid

A dial in place of a switch. Instead of jumping from 0 to 1 at a threshold, it slides smoothly between them, so a small change to the inputs makes a small change to the answer rather than none or all of it.

The logistic function, an S-shaped curve running from 0 to 1. It never quite reaches either end, so a correct answer is always a confident approximation. Being smooth, it has a usable slope everywhere — which is what makes backpropagation possible.

Used in lessons 3 and 4Wikipedia

squared error

A way of scoring how wrong an answer is: take the difference between what was said and what was wanted, and square it. Squaring makes every miss count as a positive amount and makes large misses count disproportionately.

The loss whose surface the delta rule and backpropagation descend. It is smooth in the parameters, which is precisely what counting mistakes is not.

Used in lesson 4Wikipedia

step function

A switch. Below a certain point the answer is 0, at or above it the answer is 1, and there is nothing in between.

The Heaviside step. Its derivative is zero everywhere it is defined and undefined at the jump, so it offers a gradient method no information whatsoever — the reason the sigmoid replaced it.

Used in lessons 2 to 4Wikipedia

supervised learning

Learning from examples that come with their answers attached. Somebody has already gone through and marked each one, and the machine's job is to reproduce those markings and then extend them to cases nobody has marked.

Learning a mapping from inputs to targets given labelled pairs. Every lesson here is supervised; the labels are known because the examples are generated with their class decided first.

Used in lessons 1 and 2Wikipedia

symbolic AI

The tradition that treats intelligence as the manipulation of symbols standing for things in the world — sentences, rules, logical structures — rather than as numbers adjusted by experience. Its systems are built by being told things, not by being trained on them.

The mainstream of artificial intelligence research from the 1950s into the 1980s and the setting for expert systems; sometimes called GOFAI, after John Haugeland. The connectionist tradition the other lessons follow is its counterpart, and the two have exchanged prominence more than once.

Used in lesson 5Wikipedia

template

A single stored picture that a machine compares everything against, brightening where it expects one class and darkening where it expects the other. Its verdict is how well the thing in front of it lines up with that one picture.

Folding a linear unit's weights back into the shape of its input. It is the whole of what such a unit knows, and it is why moving a shape a little way across the frame can undo it.

Used in lesson 2Wikipedia

threshold

The level a unit's total has to reach before it fires. Below it the answer is no, at or above it the answer is yes.

Usually folded into the bias and the comparison made against zero, which is the same arrangement written more conveniently.

Used in lessons 1, 3 and 4Wikipedia

translation invariance

Recognising a thing no matter where in the frame it sits. People do this without noticing; a machine that has learned one fixed template does not do it at all.

Invariance of the output under a shift of the input. Minsky and Papert's group invariance theorem shows how severely a demand of this kind constrains what weights are available, and so what such a machine can tell apart.

Used in lesson 2Wikipedia

truth table

A list of every possible combination of inputs alongside the answer each one should get. With two inputs that are each on or off there are four combinations, so the table has four rows.

The complete specification of a Boolean function. It is small enough for two inputs that a claim of impossibility can be checked exhaustively rather than argued.

Used in lesson 3Wikipedia

universal approximation

The result that a network with one hidden layer, given enough units in it, can imitate essentially any sensible function as closely as you care to ask.

A statement about what such networks can represent. It says nothing about how many units are needed, and nothing whatsoever about whether training will find the arrangement — which is where the practical difficulty actually lives.

Used in lesson 3Wikipedia

vanishing gradient

What happens to the blame as it is passed back through many layers: at each step it is multiplied by something smaller than one, so by the time it reaches the early layers there is almost nothing of it left and they barely learn.

The product of many small factors in the chain rule shrinks towards zero. A sigmoid unit that has saturated near 0 or 1 contributes a particularly small factor, which is the sense in which a confident unit is a deaf one.

Used in lesson 4Wikipedia

weight

A number saying how much one input counts towards the answer. Large and positive means that input pushes hard towards yes, large and negative pushes hard towards no, and near zero means the machine is barely listening to it at all.

The coefficient an input is multiplied by before the sum. The weights, together with the bias, are the whole of what a unit has learned and the only thing training changes.

Used in lessons 1 to 4Wikipedia

weight initialisation

What the weights are set to before any learning happens. It sounds like a detail and is not: start every weight in a layer at the same value and the units in it stay identical for ever, because identical units receive identical blame and take identical steps.

Small random values are used to break that symmetry. Lesson 4 shows the failure directly — an all-zero start leaves a network that never moves at all, with a gradient that is perfectly correct and perfectly useless.

Used in lesson 4Wikipedia

XOR

Exclusive or: the answer is yes when exactly one of two inputs is on, and no when they agree, whether they agree on yes or on no. It is the or of ordinary speech, where tea or coffee offers you one and not both.

The smallest function a single unit cannot compute. Of the sixteen functions of two binary inputs, fourteen are linearly separable; XOR and its negation are the two that are not.
